Technical

Check accuracy on a made-up report

In plain words: the page invents a report with made-up patients, hides the patient information in it, and then counts how much it caught. On a made-up report of 600 rows it catches close to 100% of names, dates and numbers. That is the best case, not your miss rate: the same people wrote the generator and the detector.

Contains no real patients. For compliance: token recall per category with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval, over-redaction rate, leak check, the memo's hard cases and a layer ablation. Your own reviewed reports are the real number.

What each layer adds

Restore identities (hospital computer only)

Load a protected workbook that came out of this tool plus the session key saved with it. Codes, made-up names and shifted dates are put back. Do this only on the hospital machine. Works for protected text and screenshot files too.

What it catches and what it may miss

It catches every patient name, MRN, date of birth, account number and phone number that appears in the report's own columns, wherever they also appear in notes, in any spelling the computer can predict. It catches dates, phone numbers, emails, addresses, ages over 89, and names, places and organizations that have a clue around them (Dr., son, called, Hospital, "City, ST"). Before any download it checks its own output against the real identifiers in the file.

It may miss: an unusual surname with no clue around it ("Vanterpool called about labs"); a hospital or clinic name you have not added to Always remove; text a screenshot reader got wrong (flagged as "Unclear in screenshot"); and small cells, where a patient on a regimen nobody else gets is identifiable regardless. Look at the flagged lines before you download. A word it left in the text and cannot explain (not an ordinary or medical word, not a common abbreviation, not something it replaced) is listed as an unexplained word: the result then says "Review required" and downloads stay off until you have hidden each one or said it is fine, or pressed "I have reviewed this". A name that is also an ordinary word (Will, Rose, June) is not caught this way.

What this tool stores

Only three kinds of things, in this browser's local storage on this computer: which column is which for each report layout (header names only), your Always remove / Never remove word lists, and the Advanced settings. Never names, record numbers or dates of birth: a patient list you paste or choose stays in this tab's memory and is gone when the tab closes. The session key is written only when you press its button and only to where you save it. Nothing is sent anywhere: the page's security policy forbids every network connection.

If compliance requires Safe Harbor, set Dates to Remove under Advanced. The default shifts dates by a secret per-patient number of days so treatment intervals stay correct; the key holds the offsets.

Made-up names are never reused. One allocator per session hands out every made-up name, for patients, doctors, relatives, streets and organizations alike, and a saved key seeds it, so a name in the key is never given to a second person and Restore identities is one-to-one.

How it works

A single HTML file that de-identifies Epic schedule and ordering exports on a locked-down work computer: no install, no admin rights, no network. It implements the architecture from the PHI detection research memo, minus the trained encoder:

  1. Schema pass. Each column is classified (identifier, date, age, free text, keep, drop) from its header and values; you confirm once per report layout.
  2. Census dictionary. Every patient's name variants, MRN, DOB, account and phone from the structured columns, plus provider names, become an exact-match detector. For the identifiers that matter most this is recall by construction. A session-wide patient registry links the same person across tables, pasted text and screenshots (by MRN, then name + DOB, then a close name).
  3. Tier 0 rules. MRN pattern learned from your MRN column, phones with extensions, emails, SSNs, URLs, street addresses, ZIPs, ages 90+, and dates in every format Epic emits.
  4. Tier 1-lite. A lexicon and cue detector for names, organizations and locations: honorifics, credentials, relationship words ("pt's son Mark"), contact verbs, facility words and acronyms, "City, ST". Clinical eponyms (Hodgkin, Bell, Graves…) and drug names are protected.
  5. Post-processing. Overlap resolution, merging, and document-wide propagation of every detected name.
  6. Hide in plain sight. Realistic surrogate names consistent per person, per-patient date shifting that preserves intervals, age banding, 3-digit ZIPs, fake organizations. The session key is produced separately.
  7. Self-check and audit. Every output cell is scanned for the real identifiers in the census before download is allowed. Each row records what was found and by which layer; the date offset lives only in the key.
  8. Screenshots. The optional larger build reads the text of a screenshot in the page (Tesseract OCR, offline). Only the recognised text is protected; the image is never written anywhere and must not leave the hospital.

Limits you should know

  • Out-of-lexicon names with no cue ("Vanterpool called") are the known gap. The memo's fix is a compact clinical transformer (Tier 1). This file has the slot for it; it is not in this build.
  • Local facility names without a generic word (ECMC, Kenmore Mercy) are caught only through patterns or your "Always remove" list. Fill that list from your own referral sources.
  • Relative dates ("next Tues") and years alone are not identifiers under Safe Harbor and are left as is.
  • Small cells. A patient on a regimen nobody else gets is identifiable regardless of redaction. The tool cannot see that; the expert-determination memo must.
  • Synthetic scores are not your miss rate. Build the 200–300 row gold set on real reports, two annotators, and report the worst category with its confidence interval.
